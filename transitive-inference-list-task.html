Our research

Transitive Inference List Task

How do we combine separately learned relationships into a larger, organized whole?

In the traditional transitive inference task, participants learn relationships between pairs of stimuli and are then tested on relationships they have never encountered directly. The transitive inference list (TIL) task takes a different approach: participants learn several short sequences of images, some of which overlap.

For example, participants might learn three sequences:

A → B → C
C → D → E
E → F → G

Each sequence is learned separately, with participants selecting the images in the correct order. Because the sequences share items, they can potentially be combined into one longer sequence: A → B → C → D → E → F → G.

The critical question is whether participants recognize these connections and use them to make judgments about relationships between items from different sequences.

What we investigate

What Have We Found?

In our initial study with human participants, we found that awareness of the overlapping items mattered. Participants who recognized the connections between sequences behaved as though they had integrated them into a single longer list. Those who did not recognize the connections tended to rely on the positions of items within their original short sequences.

Importantly, the task allows us to distinguish these strategies by examining choices on test pairs for which they predict different responses.

We are also extending this work to rhesus monkeys in collaboration with Matt Babb and Sarah Brosnan. This research will help us investigate how nonhuman primates learn and combine overlapping sequences, and whether they use strategies similar to those observed in humans.

What's next

Where Are We Going Next?

One of our next questions is how performance on the TIL task relates to performance on the traditional transitive inference task. Do the two tasks measure similar relational abilities, or do they depend on different learning and reasoning processes?
