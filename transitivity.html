Transitivity: Is It the Same as Transitive Inference?

If A equals B, and B equals C, then A must equal C. This is an example of transitivity, a fundamental property of many relationships.

Sounds familiar? It should! Transitive inference tasks also involve learning relationships among items. But here's the interesting part: transitive inference and transitivity are not necessarily the same thing.

In a typical transitive inference task, animals learn a series of overlapping pairs, such as A+B−, B+C−, C+D−, and D+E−, and are then tested on new combinations.

In a transitivity task, animals learn to match pictures. For example, when A1 is presented, the correct choice is B1; when B1 is presented, the correct choice is C1. At test, we present A1, but the choices are now C1 and C2. If the animal responds transitively, it should select C1.

What we investigate

Our Research

Do these two tasks measure the same underlying cognitive ability, or do they depend on different learning and decision-making processes?

Surprisingly, despite decades of research, we don't yet have a clear answer. Transitive inference and transitivity have largely been studied separately, making direct comparisons difficult.

At CoPAL, we're investigating both tasks to understand whether success in one predicts success in the other, and what that might tell us about relational reasoning in humans and other animals.
