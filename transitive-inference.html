Transitive Inference: Reasoning Without Words

Can animals reason without language?

Imagine that Mary is taller than Ann, and Ann is taller than Kate. Even if you've never seen Mary and Kate standing together, you can figure out that Mary must be taller than Kate. This ability to infer an unfamiliar relationship from previously learned relationships is called transitive inference (TI).

But can animals do the same thing? To investigate this question, we train animals to discriminate between overlapping pairs of stimuli: A+ B−, B+ C−, C+ D−, D+ E−.

Here, the plus sign indicates a rewarded choice, and the minus sign indicates a choice that is not rewarded.

Once the animals have learned these pairs, we present a new combination: B versus D. Both stimuli have been rewarded in some training pairs and not rewarded in others. Yet animals often choose B over D, consistent with the inferred relationship B > C > D.

The interesting question is how they arrive at that choice.

What we investigate

Our Research

Can reinforcement history explain transitive inference?

One possibility is that animals simply learn how rewarding each stimulus has been and choose whichever has acquired greater associative strength. If so, their choices should be predictable from their reinforcement history.

Our computational modeling of pigeon data showed that predictions from reinforcement-based models were inconsistent with the pigeons' actual choices. We subsequently confirmed these findings using rhesus monkey data collected by our collaborators Rob Hampton and Reggie Gazes.

Simply put, reinforcement-based models cannot adequately account for the patterns of choices animals make in transitive inference tasks.

This suggests that something more than learned reinforcement values contributes to their performance.

Does the hippocampus play a role?

The hippocampus is a brain structure involved in learning and memory. But is it necessary for transitive inference? Rodent research suggests that it is, but what about pigeons?

Our research with pigeons revealed an intriguing result: hippocampal lesions impaired transitive inference performance in birds that appeared to rely on inference-based strategies, but not in birds whose choices were better explained by reinforcement-based strategies.

In other words, pigeons can approach the same task in different ways, and the importance of the hippocampus depends on the strategy they use.

Do humans need to understand what they've learned?

Humans can also perform nonverbal transitive inference tasks, sometimes without being able to explain the relationships they've learned.

Our research suggests that awareness of the relationships among stimuli may be important for consistently successful performance. People who recognize the underlying order tend to perform more reliably than those who do not. However, some individuals who are unaware of the underlying relationships still perform very accurately during testing, and we do not yet understand why others struggle without this awareness.

This raises an interesting question: how much of transitive inference depends on explicitly understanding relationships, and how much can emerge from learning without conscious awareness?
